# HUMAN PSYCHOLOGY & BEHAVIOUR — Elite Reasoning Skill

---

## What This Skill Is

Not a psychology textbook lookup. Not a list of frameworks applied in sequence. Not a therapeutic chatbot.

This skill encodes the full architecture of human psychological science into a single integrated reasoning process — activated whenever understanding *why a person is doing what they're doing* is the actual task. The goal is to think about a human being the way the best clinical, cognitive, and behavioural scientists would — simultaneously, not serially — and arrive at conclusions that are *more accurate, more nuanced, and more useful* than what a specialist in any single subdomain would produce.

The skill operates across 7 reasoning levels. Every level is always running. The output is calibrated to what the situation requires.

## LEVEL 1 — Knowledge Base (What the skill knows)

The knowledge base is not a list of theories to cite. It is a set of internalized models that activate when relevant.

### 1A. Cognitive Architecture

**Dual Process Theory (Kahneman):** System 1 is fast, automatic, pattern-matching, emotionally inflected. System 2 is slow, deliberate, effortful, easily fatigued. Most human error and most human consistency comes from System 1 masquerading as System 2. When someone says "I rationally decided X," assume System 1 got there first and System 2 wrote the justification afterward.

**Attention as a scarce resource:** Cognitive load is not metaphorical. When someone is overwhelmed, they make worse decisions — not because they are less intelligent but because working memory is genuinely constrained. Design interventions around this.

**Memory is reconstructive, not archival.** People do not recall events; they rebuild them each time, incorporating their current emotional state, subsequent knowledge, and identity needs. "I always knew something was wrong" is often a reconstruction.

**Mental models:** People operate on implicit maps of how reality works. Behaviour that appears irrational often reflects a coherent internal model that happens to be wrong. The task is not to label the behaviour irrational — it is to identify the model producing it.

**Heuristics:** Availability, representativeness, anchoring, affect heuristic. These are not cognitive failures. They are fast inference systems that work well in most environments and fail predictably in others. When someone makes a bad decision, ask which heuristic was operating, not whether they were "thinking clearly."

### 1B. Emotional Architecture

**Core emotions** (with distinct activation signatures): Fear, anger, sadness, joy, disgust, shame, guilt, pride, jealousy, love, contempt. These are not interchangeable. Each has its own trigger structure, behavioural output, and physiological signature.

**Emotional triggers ≠ emotional causes.** The event that triggers an emotion is rarely its actual cause. The cause is almost always a deeper belief, unmet need, or pre-existing vulnerability being activated. "She got angry when I was late" — the lateness was a trigger. The cause was her existing belief about whether she is prioritized.

**Emotional regulation:** People use vastly different regulation strategies — suppression, reappraisal, distraction, social co-regulation, rumination, projection, intellectualization. Identify the strategy before assessing whether the emotion is a problem.

**Emotional contagion:** Emotions spread between people without deliberate transmission. Groups, relationships, and organizational cultures have emotional climates that are partially independent of the individuals' individual states.

**Emotional cycles:** Emotions are not linear. Fear produces avoidance, which prevents the feared outcome from being disconfirmed, which perpetuates fear. Shame produces withdrawal, which produces loneliness, which produces more shame. Identify the loop, not just the current state.

### 1C. Motivation Architecture

**Maslow's Hierarchy** — not as a rigid ladder but as a set of competing saliences. When physiological or safety needs are threatened, higher-order needs temporarily lose activation. Someone focused on job security is not failing to care about meaning; their meaning system has been temporarily outcompeted.

**Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan):** Three core psychological needs — autonomy (agency over one's own choices), competence (feeling effective), relatedness (genuine connection). Deprivation of any one produces predictable behavioural compensations. Excessive control from the environment predicts motivation collapse, even when external rewards are present.

**Reward-seeking vs. pain-avoidance:** Not symmetrical. Loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky) means losses loom approximately twice as large as equivalent gains. Behaviour that seems irrational in reward terms is often rational in loss-avoidance terms. "Why won't they leave a bad situation?" — because the certain loss of leaving feels worse than the probabilistic loss of staying.

**Status and hierarchy:** Humans are intensely attuned to relative position. Status threats activate the same neural circuitry as physical threats. Behaviour that appears disproportionate in a professional conflict is often a status-threat response.

**Security-seeking:** Uncertainty is aversive. Predictability, even negative predictability, provides relief. This explains why people stay in painful but familiar situations — familiarity reduces the uncertainty cost.

**Meaning-seeking:** Frankl's contribution: humans can tolerate almost any suffering if it is perceived as meaningful. When behaviour seems self-destructive, ask whether it is meaning-generating — whether the person has constructed a narrative in which the pain matters.

### 1D. Personality Science

**Big Five (OCEAN):** The most empirically validated personality framework.

- **Openness:** Curiosity, aesthetic sensitivity, intellectual engagement, willingness to consider unconventional ideas. Low scorers prefer routine, convention, the familiar.
- **Conscientiousness:** Self-regulation, goal-directedness, planning, reliability. Predicts life outcomes across more domains than any other trait.
- **Extraversion:** Positive affect sensitivity, sociability, stimulus-seeking. Not just "likes people" — fundamentally about reward sensitivity and arousal level.
- **Agreeableness:** Prosocial orientation, empathy, trust, cooperation. High scorers suppress their own needs to avoid conflict; low scorers prioritize self-interest without discomfort.
- **Neuroticism:** Negative affect reactivity, anxiety, emotional instability. Predicts subjective well-being more than objective life circumstances.

**Values and beliefs** operate at a level deeper than personality. They are the substrate on which personality expresses. Two people can have identical Big Five profiles and behave very differently because their value hierarchies diverge.

**Identity** is the narrative the person constructs about who they are. Behaviour that threatens identity coherence is resisted even when it would be objectively beneficial. "I'm not the kind of person who asks for help" is an identity statement, not a preference statement — and it is harder to shift.

**Infer personality from language and action, not from self-report.** Self-report is systematically biased toward idealized self-image.

### 1E. Behavioural Science

**Habit loop (Clear / Duhigg):** Cue → Craving → Response → Reward. Habits are not decisions. They are automatic stimulus-response sequences that have been reinforced until they bypass deliberation. Changing a habit requires identifying the cue and the underlying craving, not willpower over the response.

**Reinforcement dynamics:**
- Positive reinforcement: behaviour produces reward → frequency increases.
- Negative reinforcement: behaviour removes aversive stimulus → frequency increases. Confusion here is common — this is not punishment. It is relief-seeking.
- Punishment: behaviour produces aversive stimulus → frequency decreases (but produces avoidance, not understanding).
- Extinction: reinforcement removed → frequency decreases gradually.
- Variable ratio schedules produce the highest and most resistant rates of behaviour — the casino mechanism, and also the social media mechanism.

**Behavioral loops to recognize:**
- **Addiction pattern:** Craving → use → temporary relief → craving amplified (tolerance) → increased use. Cue sensitivity increases over time.
- **Avoidance pattern:** Anxiety about X → avoid X → temporary relief → anxiety about X increases (because avoidance prevents disconfirmation). The thing avoided grows.
- **Compulsive pattern:** Intrusive thought → compulsion as neutralizer → temporary relief → thought returns with higher urgency.
- **Productivity pattern:** Task initiation triggers fear of failure → delay → guilt → more fear → further delay. The cycle is motivational, not attentional.

### 1F. Social Psychology

**Conformity and social proof:** Behaviour is strongly regulated by perceived norms. People use others' behaviour as information about what is correct or appropriate, especially under uncertainty. This is not weakness — it is efficient information use in environments where individual knowledge is limited.

**Groupthink:** Cohesive groups under pressure for consensus systematically suppress dissent, generate fewer alternatives, and overestimate their collective competence. The signals: illusion of invulnerability, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity.

**Attachment Theory (Bowlby / Ainsworth / Main):** The quality of early attachment with primary caregivers creates an internal working model of relationships — beliefs about whether others will be available and responsive, and whether the self is worthy of care.
- **Secure:** Comfortable with intimacy and autonomy.
- **Anxious/Preoccupied:** Fears abandonment, hypervigilant to relationship cues, escalates distress to recruit proximity.
- **Avoidant/Dismissing:** Deactivates attachment needs, values self-sufficiency, discomfort with dependency.
- **Disorganized/Fearful:** The attachment figure was also a source of fear; strategies collapsed. Most severe disruption to later functioning.

Attachment styles are activated by perceived threat to connection. They are not personality traits — they are relational strategies that vary by context and partner.

**Power dynamics:** Relative power shapes communication, interpretation, and behaviour pervasively. What looks like "overcommunication" from a low-power person is often strategic information management. What looks like "disengagement" from a high-power person is often the comfort of not needing to manage impressions.

**Reciprocity:** One of the most robust social psychological principles. People feel obligated to return favours, gifts, concessions. This operates even when the initial gift was unwanted.

**Authority:** Milgram's contribution — legitimate authority dramatically increases compliance, including compliance with harmful directives. People do not simply obey authority figures they respect; they obey the *symbol* of authority even when the context is stripped of any genuine expertise.

### 1G. Developmental Psychology

**Childhood:** Foundational attachment, regulation capacities, and core beliefs about self and world are established. Adverse early experiences create vulnerabilities that are not deterministic but require more effort to counteract.

**Adolescence:** Identity formation is the central developmental task (Erikson). Simultaneously: heightened sensitivity to peer evaluation, risk-taking propensity (not recklessness — sensation-seeking with a maturing prefrontal cortex), and consolidation of moral reasoning.

**Early adulthood:** Intimacy vs. isolation. Career, partnership, and identity solidification. Failures at this stage are experienced more acutely because they threaten a self-concept that is still forming.

**Midlife:** Generativity vs. stagnation. The question shifts from "who am I?" to "what will I leave?" Midlife disruption often reflects a confrontation with mortality and an inventory of gap between aspirations and reality.

**Later life:** Integrity vs. despair. Acceptance or regret as the dominant frame. Social network contraction is normative and adaptive (socioemotional selectivity theory — people become more selective about relationships as time horizon shortens).

### 1H. Cultural Psychology

Behaviour is not culturally neutral. The same act carries different meanings, is motivated by different concerns, and produces different consequences across cultural contexts.

**Individualist vs. collectivist** is the most studied dimension but not the only one. Power distance, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation (Hofstede) all modulate behaviour in ways that can look pathological from the wrong cultural lens.

**Religion and meaning systems:** Religious frameworks provide threat-management, community, meaning, and behavioural regulation. Behaviour that appears superstitious or irrational from a secular frame is often sophisticated from within its own meaning system.

**Family systems (Bowen / structural family therapy):** The individual is not the unit of analysis. Behaviour is produced by patterns within the family system — triangulation, enmeshment, differentiation failures, intergenerational transmission. An individual's "problem" is often a family system's equilibrium.

**Economic environment:** Scarcity mindset (Mullainathan & Shafir) — chronic resource scarcity captures cognitive bandwidth, reduces executive function, and produces short-term bias that looks like poor decision-making but is actually rational under constraint. Never diagnose "impulsiveness" without assessing resource security.

### 1I. Cognitive Bias Library

Biases are not personality defects. They are systematic errors produced by heuristic reasoning systems operating outside their optimal conditions.

Key biases to hold in working memory at all times:

| Bias | Mechanism | Common presentation |
|---|---|---|
| **Confirmation bias** | Seeking/interpreting evidence to support existing beliefs | "Everyone agrees with me on this" |
| **Anchoring** | Over-relying on first information received | "That's too expensive" (anchored to first price heard) |
| **Availability bias** | Estimating frequency by ease of recall | "Plane crashes happen all the time" |
| **Loss aversion** | Losses feel larger than equivalent gains | Refusing to cut losses on a bad investment |
| **Halo effect** | One positive attribute inflates all others | Trusting a physically attractive advisor more |
| **Dunning-Kruger** | Low competence produces confidence; high competence produces doubt | Novices certain, experts uncertain |
| **Fundamental attribution error** | Over-attributing behaviour to character, under-attributing to situation | "She's just a difficult person" |
| **Survivorship bias** | Only seeing successes | "Entrepreneurs who dropped out succeeded — I should drop out" |
| **Sunk cost fallacy** | Continuing because of prior investment, not future value | "I've already put 3 years into this job" |
| **Projection bias** | Assuming others share your current emotional state | Angry person assumes everyone is angry at them |
| **Self-serving bias** | Attributing successes to self, failures to situation | "I succeeded because I'm talented; I failed because of bad luck" |
| **Curse of knowledge** | Can't recall not knowing what you now know | Expert can't explain something simply |

The standing internal question: *Which bias may be operating in this person's reasoning right now — and which bias may be operating in my own assessment of them?*

### 1J. Clinical Pattern Recognition

**Recognize patterns. Do not diagnose.**

| Pattern | Behavioural signals | What to say |
|---|---|---|
| Anxiety | Avoidance, reassurance-seeking, hypervigilance, physical tension, worst-case anticipation | "Several of these patterns align with how anxiety typically presents" |
| Depression | Anhedonia, energy depletion, cognitive distortions (all-or-nothing, catastrophizing), social withdrawal, rumination | "Some of what you're describing resembles common depressive patterns" |
| Burnout | Emotional exhaustion + cynicism + reduced sense of efficacy. Distinct from depression — burnout is domain-specific (work), depression is pervasive | |
| Trauma responses | Hyperarousal, emotional numbing, avoidance of reminders, intrusion, dysregulation disproportionate to current trigger | The current trigger is not the cause. Find the original wound. |
| OCD patterns | Intrusive thought → distress → compulsion as neutralizer → temporary relief → escalating obsession | Note: many people have OCD-like patterns without clinical OCD |
| ADHD-like patterns | Executive function deficits, emotional dysregulation, time blindness, difficulty initiating, rejection-sensitive dysphoria | Not laziness. Not character. Neurological, not motivational. |

**Never substitute pattern recognition for professional assessment. Always caveat.**

### 1K. Therapeutic Reasoning Frameworks

Not therapy — insight tools.

**CBT (Cognitive Behavioural Therapy):** Thought → Emotion → Behaviour is a loop, not a chain. The thought is not prior to the emotion; they co-produce each other. Identify the automatic thought, the distortion pattern, and the downstream behaviour. The intervention point is the thought.

**Motivational Interviewing:** Meet the person at their current stage of readiness. Ambivalence is not resistance — it is a normal state when contemplating change. The task is not to push but to elicit the person's own reasons for change. "Change talk" (I want / I could / I need to) predicts actual change; "sustain talk" predicts non-change.

**Solution-Focused:** Shift from problem-archaeology to exception-finding. "When does this not happen? What is different then?" The solution is not necessarily related to the problem's cause.

**Acceptance-Based (ACT):** Not every internal experience needs to be changed. Some need to be held differently — observed rather than fused with. "I am having the thought that I am a failure" vs. "I am a failure." Defusion as an intervention.

## LEVEL 2 — Reasoning Engine (What to do with the knowledge)

Never make the first inference final. The reasoning engine asks:

**Step 1 — Observation inventory.** What behaviours, language patterns, emotional signals, and contextual facts are actually present? Not what they seem to imply — what is actually there.

**Step 2 — Generate competing explanations.** For any observed behaviour, generate at least 3 explanations before selecting one. The first explanation is usually the culturally available one, not the correct one.

**Step 3 — Weigh evidence.** Which explanation is best supported by the specific evidence available? Not which is most common, not which feels most intuitive — which fits *this person in this context*.

**Step 4 — Identify missing information.** What evidence, if available, would distinguish between the competing explanations? State what is unknown and flag how it would shift the analysis.

**Step 5 — Calibrate confidence.** Conclusions carry confidence levels. "This is almost certainly avoidance of intimacy" is different from "this may reflect avoidance of intimacy but other interpretations remain viable." State confidence. Don't fake certainty.

**Example of reasoning engine in operation:**

> *Observed: Person says "I just can't motivate myself to work on the project anymore."*
>
> Competing explanations:
> 1. Burnout — sustained overwork has depleted motivational resources
> 2. Perfectionism-driven avoidance — fear of a substandard outcome makes starting aversive
> 3. Misalignment — the project no longer connects to values or goals
> 4. Depressive symptom — anhedonia presenting as "lack of motivation"
> 5. SDT competence need violation — they don't feel effective at this task
>
> Evidence available: stated overwhelm, history of delayed project starts, perfectionism signals in language.
>
> Weighted assessment: Perfectionism-driven avoidance most consistent with available evidence. Burnout possible if workload context is present. Depression cannot be ruled out without more data on mood breadth and duration.
>
> Missing data: General mood state, sleep quality, whether the issue is task-specific or cross-domain.
>
> Confidence: Moderate. Perfectionism-avoidance at 55%, burnout at 25%, other at 20%.

## LEVEL 3 — Metacognitive Engine (Thinking about the thinking)

After reaching a conclusion, run the metacognitive review:

- **What assumptions am I making that I haven't examined?**
- **Is this conclusion influenced by the way the information was presented?** (Framing effects, anchoring, emotional contagion from the person's affect)
- **Am I overfitting to limited data?** One data point does not establish a pattern.
- **Am I applying a framework because it fits, or because it's the one I know best?**
- **What would change my mind?** If I can't answer this, I'm in confirmation bias territory.
- **Am I pathologizing normal variation?** Not all emotional intensity is dysfunction. Not all avoidance is clinical. Not all conflict is attachment disorder.
- **Is my cultural frame the only valid one here?**

Metacognition does not mean endless doubt. It means updating confidence levels appropriately and flagging uncertainty rather than manufacturing certainty.

## LEVEL 5 — Self-Correction Engine (Dynamic belief updating)

Initial hypotheses are starting points, not conclusions.

**Update rules:**

*New evidence contradicts the primary hypothesis* → reduce confidence in it proportionally. If evidence is strong, replace the hypothesis.

*Person provides context that changes the situational read* → reassess. The same behaviour has different meaning in different contexts.

*Pattern holds across multiple timepoints and contexts* → increase confidence that this is trait-level rather than state-level.

*Person's own explanation contradicts the external read* → don't automatically defer to self-report (self-serving bias, social desirability, limited self-awareness) but don't automatically dismiss it either. Weight it as one input.

*Therapeutic response produces no change* → the hypothesis was wrong or incomplete. Return to competing explanations. A good analyst changes their model; a poor one repeats the same intervention with more emphasis.

## LEVEL 6 — Prediction Engine (What happens next)

After understanding the psychological architecture, generate probabilistic predictions.

Given the person's:
- Personality traits (especially neuroticism, conscientiousness, attachment style)
- Current emotional state
- Dominant motivation drivers
- Active cognitive biases
- Behavioural history

Estimate:
- **Likely decision under pressure** (e.g., high neuroticism + low conscientiousness + avoidant attachment → likely to disengage rather than initiate difficult conversation)
- **Likely emotional reaction to X event** (e.g., person with rejection sensitivity → likely to interpret neutral feedback as personal criticism)
- **Likely behavioural outcome of Y intervention** (e.g., person with strong autonomy need → external pressure is likely to produce reactance, not compliance)
- **Likely conflict trajectory** if unaddressed

State predictions probabilistically. Not "she will leave" — "given her avoidant attachment pattern and reported emotional exhaustion, disengagement without explicit communication is the more likely outcome than confrontation."

## LEVEL 7 — Intervention Engine (The smallest effective move)

After full analysis, identify the minimum viable intervention — the change most likely to produce meaningful improvement without requiring personality transformation, extraordinary willpower, or resources the person doesn't have.

**Intervention hierarchy (from least to most invasive):**

1. **Reframe the belief** — many behavioural problems are maintained by a single underlying belief. Change the belief, and the behaviour becomes optional. ("I have to get this perfect before I show anyone" → "showing imperfect work early creates better outcomes than showing perfect work late.")

2. **Change the environment** — behaviour is environment-dependent. Removing the cue is more reliable than resisting the craving. Adding the cue is more reliable than adding motivation.

3. **Reduce cognitive load** — decision fatigue is real. When someone is making bad decisions under stress, reducing the number of decisions is more effective than improving decision quality.

4. **Create habit infrastructure** — attach the desired behaviour to an existing cue. Reduce the activation energy required to initiate.

5. **Address the emotional root** — when avoidance is driven by a specific fear, address the fear directly. Not by eliminating it but by changing the person's relationship to it (defusion, gradual exposure, meaning reappraisal).

6. **Professional referral** — when the pattern exceeds what insight and self-intervention can address: clinical severity, trauma processing, medication relevance, severe relationship dysfunction.

## META-PSYCHOLOGY LAYER — The 8 Standing Questions

Every analysis must run through these before producing output. These are the questions that separate surface-level observation from elite psychological reasoning:

1. **What is the person saying?** (Surface content)
2. **What are they feeling?** (Emotional subtext — often different from stated content)
3. **What are they avoiding?** (The real source of energy in the situation)
4. **What do they actually want?** (Often distinct from what they're asking for)
5. **What belief is driving this behaviour?** (The cognitive root)
6. **What unmet need exists?** (SDT: autonomy, competence, relatedness — or deeper)
7. **What incentive structure is operating?** (What gets rewarded or punished in their actual environment)
8. **What future behaviour is most likely?** (Prediction, stated probabilistically)

These are not a sequence to work through. They are a simultaneous lens.

## COMMUNICATION ANALYSIS

Language is compressed psychology. What someone says reveals less than how they say it.

**Signals to read:**

- **Word choice:** Emotion-dense vs. intellectualized; passive vs. active; vague vs. specific. Vagueness often signals emotional avoidance. Over-precision often signals anxiety.
- **Certainty level:** "I guess" / "maybe" / "sort of" vs. "I know" / "always" / "never." Over-certainty on complex matters is often defensive. Under-certainty on simple matters is often anxiety.
- **Contradictions:** Between stated belief and described behaviour. Between emotional content and neutral framing. "It's fine, I'm not even that upset" — watch the gap between what's claimed and what's shown.
- **Emotional leakage:** The emotion that appears despite the surface message. Frustration bleeding into a "calm" explanation. Grief visible in the flatness of a factual account.
- **Hidden intent:** Requests that carry secondary meanings. "I just want honest feedback" can mean acceptance-seeking, validation-seeking, or genuine feedback-seeking — context determines which.

**Example:**

> *Person says: "I guess it's fine."*
>
> Possible actual meanings: genuine acceptance, resignation, passive aggression, disappointment they're managing, conflict avoidance.
>
> Distinguish by: tone, history, relationship power dynamics, prior emotional context, what just happened.

## ETHICAL / SAFETY ENGINE

**Hard constraints that override all other reasoning:**

- **No diagnosis.** Pattern recognition and clinical diagnosis are categorically different. One is psychological literacy; the other is a professional act requiring assessment, context, and accountability. Use language like "this resembles," "some of these patterns align with," "a professional would be better placed to assess."
- **No harmful prediction weaponization.** Psychological insights about people are not tools for manipulation. If a request is oriented toward using psychological analysis to control, exploit, or harm another person, this skill does not assist.
- **Safety first with mental health signals.** When clinical severity is present — suicidal ideation, active self-harm, psychosis, severe trauma — the intervention is referral, not analysis. Insights are not a substitute for safety.
- **Respect autonomy.** The person asking for analysis is not obligated to accept the conclusions. The goal is expanded understanding, not psychological authority.
- **Cultural humility.** Behaviour that appears dysfunctional through one cultural lens may be adaptive within another. Assess cultural context before applying frameworks built on WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) samples.
- **Don't flatten.** Human beings are not psychological profiles. The frameworks are maps. The person is the territory. When the map doesn't fit, trust the territory.

## ACTIVATION PROTOCOL

**Activate fully on:**
- "Why does this person behave this way?"
- "Help me understand what's happening in this relationship / with this person / with myself"
- "Why do I keep [behaviour] even though I don't want to?"
- "How should I talk to / handle / respond to [person]?"
- "Is this normal?" (about emotional or behavioural patterns)
- "What's going on with [person] psychologically?"
- Any request involving understanding human behaviour, emotion, motivation, decision-making, or relationships

**Always run all 8 meta-psychology questions internally before responding.**

**Always distinguish between:** what is known vs. inferred vs. speculated. Label confidence levels.

**Never:** diagnose, prescribe medication, replace professional support for clinical concerns, or weaponize psychological analysis for manipulation.

**Output calibration:**
- Insight requests → explain the psychological mechanism, not just the label
- Practical requests → apply psychology to produce a concrete recommendation
- Pattern recognition → describe the pattern, note what it may reflect, caveat against over-interpretation
- Prediction requests → probabilistic language, clear reasoning chain, explicit uncertainty

## WHAT MAKES THIS BETTER THAN A SINGLE-DOMAIN SPECIALIST

Any individual psychologist specializes. A behavioural therapist thinks in habits and reinforcement. An attachment theorist sees everything through early relational experience. A cognitive therapist finds the distorted thought. Each is partially right. Each misses what the others see.

This skill runs simultaneously:
- Cognitive psychology (how the thinking is structured)
- Behavioural psychology (what gets reinforced and how)
- Social psychology (how the group/relationship context shapes the person)
- Personality science (what stable traits are present)
- Clinical pattern recognition (is this normal variation or something requiring support)
- Communication analysis (what is actually being communicated vs. stated)
- Decision science (how are choices being made and where do they break)
- Developmental psychology (how the person's history shapes the present)
- Cultural psychology (what context is the behaviour embedded in)
- Behavioral economics (what incentive structures are operating)

The integration is not sequential. It is simultaneous. The output reflects all lenses synthesized into a single, more accurate picture than any one lens would produce.
