# Viral Hook Generator

The hook's job is to earn the next 1–3 seconds of attention. It can't deliver value alone; it sets up the content that does. A great hook with weak content burns once and trains the audience to scroll past you. A great hook with great content compounds.

## Core principle

**Hooks promise; content delivers.** A hook that overpromises trains the audience to discount your future hooks. A hook that earns its content trains the audience to give you attention. The skill writes hooks calibrated to what the actual content can deliver — not maximally clickable hooks that the body can't sustain.

## When to use

| Situation | Activate? |
|---|---|
| Short-form video script needs a hook | Yes |
| Twitter / LinkedIn thread opener | Yes |
| YouTube title + thumbnail concept | Yes |
| Email subject line | Yes |
| Newsletter subject + preview text | Yes |
| Article H1 / blog post title | Yes |
| Hook for content the user can't substantiate | Refuse |
| Outrage-bait or division-bait | Refuse |
| Hook for misleading / scam content | Refuse |
| Long-form opening (book, essay, talk) | Reconsider — different craft, less "scroll-stopping," more "earning trust" |

## Hook archetypes

Hooks fall into a small number of shapes. Knowing them produces variety quickly.

### 1. The contrarian thesis
States the popular wisdom, contradicts it, promises evidence.

> "Everyone's optimizing their funnel. The teams winning right now aren't."
> "All your favorite productivity advice is for people who already have time."
> "The fastest way to grow your audience is to lose followers."

Works because: scrolls past consensus; promises the surprising.

Risk: only works if you can actually substantiate. Contrarian-just-to-be-contrarian erodes credibility fast.

### 2. The specific stat
A number that's surprising, with the implied promise of explanation.

> "We tested 47 cold email subject lines. The winner: a single word."
> "The page on our site that converts at 38% has no images, no testimonials, no buttons."
> "$2,400 in ad spend, $0 in sales. Here's what we did wrong."

Works because: specific numbers signal real testing; reader expects the explanation.

Risk: numbers must be honest. Round-numbered fake stats ("80% of marketers...") are detectable and demote you.

### 3. The reveal
States a surprising private thing you'll explain.

> "We almost shut down 60 days before our breakout post."
> "Our biggest client paid us $0 for the first 8 months."
> "The agency name wasn't even mine. Here's how I bought it."

Works because: insider information that's specifically yours; can't be commodified.

Risk: must actually be true and worth telling.

### 4. The question that hits a nerve
Asks something the audience has wondered, never satisfactorily answered.

> "Why does your competitor's worse product outsell yours?"
> "What do top creators know that you don't?"
> "When does 'consistent posting' start to actually work?"

Works because: unfinished business in the reader's mind.

Risk: audience-aware hook — needs you to know the audience's actual unfinished business, not generic.

### 5. The listicle promise
Promises N specific things; reader scans for the count and expects density.

> "5 emails that closed $80K each in 2025."
> "11 things I'd do differently after 100 product launches."
> "3 mistakes that killed our first two startups."

Works because: signals scannable structure; commits to specificity.

Risk: listicles oversold; reader expects substance, not 5 vague platitudes.

### 6. The cold-open story
Drops you into a specific moment, mid-action.

> "I sent the contract at 11:53pm. By 7am the next morning, the deal was dead."
> "She walked into the meeting holding a printout of every Slack message I'd sent that quarter."
> "The CEO said 'no' three times before we figured out the right question to ask."

Works because: narrative tension; reader needs resolution.

Risk: cold opens that don't pay off feel like cheap fiction. The story has to actually happen and matter.

### 7. The before/after gap
States the gap between starting and ending state, promising the bridge.

> "From 200 monthly visits to 80,000 in 90 days. Here's what changed."
> "Our team of 4 doing the work of 14. Here's the system."
> "$0 in pipeline to $1.2M in 6 months. Without ads."

Works because: outcome-driven; reader wants the method.

Risk: works only if numbers are real and method is teachable. Hype-cycle gap claims ("$0 to $1M in 30 days") are dismissed.

### 8. The challenged norm
Calls out a practice everyone does and questions it.

> "Stop sending 'just checking in' emails."
> "Your discovery call template is the reason you're losing deals."
> "Brainstorming meetings produce worse ideas than working alone. Here's why."

Works because: implicates the reader's behavior; creates curiosity to defend or learn.

Risk: must back the challenge with substance, not just contrarian for show.

### 9. The teaching frame
Positions you as having the lesson the reader wants.

> "The 4 questions that should be in every sales call."
> "How to write a cold email that gets replies, in 6 lines."
> "What to say when a prospect ghosts you."

Works because: action-oriented; promises immediate utility.

Risk: must be actually useful, not generic advice repackaged.

### 10. The taboo / forbidden
States a thing people don't usually admit, that's relatable.

> "I don't actually use my own product as much as I claim."
> "Most of my clients have terrible websites. I haven't told them."
> "I post on LinkedIn for the algorithm, not for connection."

Works because: candor signals trustworthiness; relatable shame.

Risk: this is a trust-building hook; if the body retreats from the candor, it backfires.

## Format-specific considerations

### TikTok / Reels / Shorts (video, 30–90s)
- Hook = first 1–3 seconds
- Visual + audio + text overlay all working
- Pattern interrupt: motion, color, sound, expression
- Question or statement that demands the next beat
- Loop opportunity: hook can echo the ending, encouraging replay

### Twitter / X
- Hook = first tweet, must work standalone
- 280 char with line breaks
- No clickbait formatting tricks (ALL CAPS, "🚨🚨🚨", excessive punctuation) — performs worse on the algorithm now
- Specific, not abstract

### LinkedIn
- Hook = first ~150 char (above the "see more" fold)
- Conversational tone; first-person works
- One specific claim, not a teaser
- Double-line-break formatting makes thread scannable

### YouTube
- Hook = title + thumbnail + first 8 seconds of video
- Title and thumbnail carry as much weight as opening line
- Title: curiosity-gap + specificity
- Thumbnail: face / emotion + bold text + simple graphic
- First 8 seconds: deliver enough to confirm the click, set up the rest

### Email subject + preview
- Subject: 30–50 char ideal; must work alone
- Preview text (first ~90 char of email): extends the hook
- Personalization can help (name, behavior reference) but only if not creepy
- Subject + preview is a 2-piece hook; design as one

### Article H1 / blog title
- Search-aware: contains the query phrase or natural phrasing
- 50–60 char for SEO + social preview
- Specific over clever (in most cases)
- Subhead can carry curiosity-gap that title doesn't

## Workflow

### Step 1: Understand what the content delivers

Hook can't be designed without knowing what's behind it. Ask:
- What's the substance — claim, story, lesson, data, demo?
- What's the most surprising or specific element in the body?
- Who is this for — what tribe, what level of awareness?
- What action / next step does the content lead to?

### Step 2: Generate 5–10 hook variants

Across multiple archetypes. Don't over-fit to one. Diversity reveals which archetype fits the content best.

### Step 3: Cull

Filter out:
- Generic versions (could apply to any topic)
- Overpromising versions (body can't deliver)
- Vague versions (no specific noun / number / claim)
- Hooks the user wouldn't say comfortably (off-voice)

### Step 4: Refine the top 2–3

Polish. Word-by-word. Consider:
- Could this be 3 words shorter?
- Is the strongest word at the start?
- Does the rhythm scan when read aloud?
- Does it stand alone, or need context?

### Step 5: Pair with the body

Reread the body with the chosen hook on top. Does the next sentence earn the hook? If the hook promises X and the body delivers Y, fix one or the other.

## Output format

```
# Hooks for [Content piece] — [Date]

## Content summary
- What the content delivers: [key claim / story / data]
- Format: [TikTok / Twitter / LinkedIn / etc.]
- Audience: [tribe + awareness level]

## Variants

### Variant 1 (Archetype: Specific stat)
"[Hook]"
- Why it works: [...]
- Risk: [...]
- Best for: [variant of audience / placement]

### Variant 2 (Archetype: Cold-open story)
...

[Continue per variant]

## Recommended top 3
1. [Best fit + reasoning]
2. [Strong alternative + reasoning]
3. [Different archetype to test]

## Test plan (if A/B testable)
- [Variants to run]
- [Metric: opens, scroll-past rate, completion]
- [Sample size]
```

## Anti-patterns

1. ❌ Clickbait hooks the body doesn't sustain — burns trust; algorithms detect via session retention
2. ❌ Outrage-bait that exploits division for engagement — short-term lift, long-term brand damage
3. ❌ Hooks with fake numbers — "Make $10K in 7 days" — flagged + dismissed
4. ❌ Generic hooks that work for any content ("This will change everything") — performs worse than specific even on optimistic readers
5. ❌ Stuffing all 10 archetypes in one piece (a question, then a stat, then a story...) — pick one strongest opener
6. ❌ Hooks that punch down (mocking groups, mocking competitors) — short-term lift, real reputational cost
7. ❌ Hooks the brand voice can't sustain — over-edgy hook on under-edgy brand creates cognitive dissonance
8. ❌ Hooks pretending personal stories that didn't happen — manufactured authenticity is detectable
9. ❌ Caps-and-emoji formatting tricks ("🚨 BREAKING 🚨") — algorithm-suppressed on most platforms now
10. ❌ "POV:" templates without genuine POV — formulaic; high saturation
11. ❌ Hook + body mismatch where hook is interesting and body recaps — the body should expand the hook, not summarize it
12. ❌ Hooks designed for one platform deployed to another without re-tuning — TikTok hooks rarely work on LinkedIn and vice versa
13. ❌ Engagement-bait CTAs in hooks ("Tag someone who needs this" before any value delivered) — algorithmic suppression; reader irritation
14. ❌ Hooks that imply a secret / hack / shortcut for things that don't have one — disappoints, devalues
15. ❌ One-pass generation without testing — hook performance varies wildly; test variants when stakes are real

## Confidence calibration

- Hook archetype recognition and application: high
- Per-hook performance prediction: low — depends on audience, timing, platform, body delivery
- "Will this go viral": refuse to predict; viral is a tail outcome of compounding factors, not engineered from hook alone
- Format-platform fit: high
- Voice fit to user's brand: medium without samples
